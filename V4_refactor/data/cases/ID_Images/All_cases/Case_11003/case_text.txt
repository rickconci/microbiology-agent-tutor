History of Present Illness
2011 September-October Featured Case Summary: An HIV-positive man with a painful rash of three weeks’ duration.
A man with a history of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection developed a painful rash.
The patient had been in his usual state of health until three weeks before admission when an erythematous painful lesion developed on his arm. During the following weeks, skin lesions developed on his face, trunk, extremities, back and genitalia. He also developed headache, neck pain and photophobia . One week before presentation, lightheadedness and nausea occurred.
He was brought to the emergency department for evaluation. He reported some joint discomfort, without focal joint swelling or erythema. He reported no diplopia, focal weakness, numbness, dysuria, urethral discharge, chest pain, dyspnea, cough, upper respiratory symptoms, or gastrointestinal symptoms.
Past Medical History
He was diagnosed with HIV several years earlier. Two years before admission, the CD4+ T-lymphocyte count was approximately 400 cells/mm³. He had never had a history of opportunistic infections or acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) defining illnesses, and had never taken antiretroviral medications .
Medications
Medications included an over-the-counter non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug.
Allergies
He had no allergies.
Epidemiological History
He had not traveled outside of the country. He was sexually active with men and did not always practice safe sex . He denied exposure to any animals or tick bites . He reported that he had not had any sick contacts.
Physical Examination
On examination, the temperature was 99.0°F (37.2°C), blood pressure 140/88 mm Hg, pulse 109 per minute, respirations 18 per minute and oxygen saturation 99% while breathing ambient air. He appeared uncomfortable, lying flat in bed. There was evidence of white plaques on the oral mucosa, suggestive of thrush . There was neck pain with flexion . The abdomen was tender in the right upper quadrant . Numerous infiltrated papules with variable amounts of scale were scattered over the face, arms, legs, and trunk ( Figures 1 and 2 ). Many of the lesions had a central scale-crust, suggestive of prior pustular or vesicular appearance, or drainage, and several infiltrated papules on the palms had a central hemorrhagic crust. A few lesions appeared acneiform . The remainder of the examination was normal.

Studies
The hematocrit was 34.2% (reference range 41.0-53.0 for men), white cell count 4500 cells/mm³ (ref 4500-11,000), with a normal differential , and the level of CD4+ T-lymphocytes was 247 /mm³ (ref 348-1456). The serum sodium was 131 mmol/L (ref 135-145), alkaline phosphatase 1030 U/L (ref 45-115), alanine aminotransferase 76 U/L (ref 10-55) and aspartate aminotransferase 75 U/L (ref 10-40). Levels of other electrolytes and calcium and tests of renal function and coagulation were normal.
A chest radiograph revealed slightly under-inflated lungs, and was otherwise normal, with no evidence of pneumonia. An electrocardiogram was normal.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
During the first day, the temperature rose to 102.2°F (39.0°C). Acyclovir, morphine and intravenous fluids were administered. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head demonstrated mucosal thickening throughout the paranasal sinuses, symmetric prominence of the nasopharyngeal soft tissues and partial opacification of the mastoid air cells bilaterally, with prominent cervical lymph nodes. Ultrasonography of the abdomen showed splenomegaly.
The level of HIV RNA by PCR was 245,000 copies per milliliter , and testing for viral hepatitis (types B and C) was negative.
A biopsy of a lesion was performed. Pathological examination of the tissue with hematoxylin and eosin (H & E) staining, shown in Figures 3 and 4 , revealed superficial and deep plasmacytic infiltrate. (Pathology images provided by Devon C. Gimbel, MD.)

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
The serum rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test was positive at a dilution of 1:256, and testing for anti-treponemal antibody was also positive. Treponema pallidum immunohistochemical staining of a skin biopsy revealed spirochetes between keratinocytes within the epidermis ( Figure 5 ). (Pathology image provided by Devon C. Gimbel, MD.)
Serum tests for Cryptococcus , Toxoplasma , herpes simplex virus (HSV)-1 and -2 were negative. Serologic testing was consistent with past VZV infection. Skin cultures also grew two types of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus , in rare to moderate amounts. Blood cultures were sterile.
Analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) obtained by lumbar puncture revealed 11 white cells/cubic millimeter (ref 0-5 cells), with 4% neutrophils, 90% lymphocytes and 6% monocytes and 2 red cells per cubic millimeter in the 4th tube, with protein 26 mg/dl (ref 5-55) and glucose 49 mg/dl (ref 50-75). There were no organisms visible on gram stain. The CSF venereal disease research laboratory (VDRL) test was negative. Tests of the CSF for VZV, Cryptococcus , Borrelia burgdorferi and enterovirus were negative. Cultures of the CSF were negative, as was DNA amplification tests for HSV. Culture of a skin lesion for HSV and VZV was also negative.

Treatment and Followup
Acyclovir was stopped after testing for HSV was negative. Intravenous penicillin G was administered for possible neurosyphilis. There was no evidence of retinal involvement by ophthalmological examination. Urine tests for Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae nucleic acids were negative.
Elevation of the level of alkaline phosphatase was thought to be consistent with syphilitic hepatitis. The liver tests improved after treatment for syphilis.
Repeat clinical evaluation and serologic testing by RPR at months 1, 3, 6, and 12 post-treatment were planned, with a goal of a four-fold decrease in titers.
Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis and treatment with fluconazole for thrush were begun. Follow-up for initiation of anti-retroviral medications was scheduled.
Discussion
This case illustrates distinctive aspects of the presentation of secondary syphilis, including the protean nature of its rash. Though a maculopapular eruption is common in syphilis, macular, papular, psoriasiform, and pustular lesions, as in our case, are also reported [Chapel TA 1980]. The rash can involve any area of the body and most commonly begins with development of discrete macular lesions, evolving into red papules, sometimes progressing to pustules, known as pustular syphilids [Tramont 2010].
The patient also had headache and meningismus. Meningeal signs, including headache and neck stiffness, are seen in 8-40% of patients with secondary syphilis and can signal dissemination of spirochetes into the central nervous system [Merritt 1940]. Alternate manifestations of early neurosyphilis include ocular and auditory abnormalities, dysfunction of other cranial nerves, and stroke [CDC 2007]. Any of these findings in a patient with syphilis warrants a lumbar puncture for evaluation. The diagnosis of neurosyphilis can be challenging, especially in patients with HIV due to baseline CSF abnormalities [Ghanem, Moore et al. 2008], but can be divided into 'definitive' by positive CSF Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL), and 'probable' by elevated white blood cell count (greater or equal to 10 cells/mm³) or protein (greater or equal to 50 mg/dL) in the absence of a positive VDRL and an alternate diagnosis [Marra 2009]. In those with a negative CSF VDRL, a treponemal antibody test can be performed; this test has increased sensitivity (approaching 100%) but less specificity (approximately 40%) [Marra et al. 2004]. In this case, there was not enough CSF available to perform a specific treponemal test. Because of the severity of the patient's meningeal symptoms and signs, a decision was made to treat him with intravenous penicillin for possible neurosyphilis.
Finally, the patient's elevated liver function tests suggested the possibility of syphilitic hepatitis.
As noted above, repeat clinical evaluation and serologic testing by RPR extending through 12 months post-treatment were planned, with a goal of a four-fold decrease in titers to confirm successful treatment [Workowski and Berman 2006]. Failure to achieve a serologic response, which might be more common in HIV-positive patients [Ghanem et al. 2007], warrants repeat evaluation and possible re-treatment.
The presenting triad of fever, headache, and rash as seen in this case warrants rapid evaluation and empiric antibiotics while diagnostic evaluation is performed. The most worrisome causes are meningococcemia, Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever, and Toxic Shock Syndrome. In this case, lack of petechiae or diffuse erythematous macules, lack of a neutrophilic pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid, and relatively prolonged duration of symptoms made these diagnoses unlikely. Other infectious etiologies, including disseminated herpesvirus infections (HSV, VZV), Coxsackie virus infection, and disseminated cryptococcal disease were considered, and ruled out. Non-infectious etiologies, including adverse drug reaction and pityriasis lichenoides et varioliformis acuta, remained in the differential until the diagnosis of secondary syphilis was confirmed.
Final Diagnosis
Secondary syphilis caused by Treponema pallidum in a patient with HIV infection.
