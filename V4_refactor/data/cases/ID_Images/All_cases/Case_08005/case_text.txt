History of Present Illness
2008 May Featured Case and Medical Student Curriculum Case Summary: An elderly woman with diabetes and lung disease developed fever and confusion.
A woman in her seventies was generally well until 3 days before admission when she reported to a family member that she felt stiffness between her clavicles. The following day, she spoke again with her family, and her conversation and behavior were thought to be normal. On the day before admission, she participated in social activities with residents at the senior housing complex in which she lived. The next afternoon, she was found in bed, confused, and was taken to another hospital by emergency medical services.
On examination, she was lethargic, confused, and somnolent. The temperature was 103.9°F (39.9°C). Results of tests for white blood cells and plasma levels of cardiac enzymes, including troponin, were reportedly elevated. Ceftriaxone (1 gram), moxifloxacin (400 mg), methylprednisolone and aspirin were administered. Computed tomography (CT) reportedly showed no evidence of acute infarction or hemorrhage. She was transferred to a tertiary care hospital.
Past Medical History
There was a history of , hypertension and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
Medications
Daily medications included hydrochlorothiazide, glimepiride, risedronate, pioglitazone, .
Allergies
She had no allergies.
Epidemiological History
She lived in a senior living facility. She had smoked 4 packs of cigarettes per day for many years , but had stopped more than 5 years earlier, and did not drink alcohol or use illicit drugs.
Four days before admission, she had attended a house party where she ate a variety of foods, and had taken some leftovers home with her. No other guest had reportedly become ill. There was no history of travel, animal exposures, or ingestion of unpasteurized milk or cheese, or raw or uncooked meat or fish.
Physical Examination
On examination, the patient was responsive, oriented and appeared mildly agitated. The temperature was 100.7°F (38.2°C) initially, and rose to 101.9°F (38.8°C). The blood pressure was 149/72 mm Hg, pulse 117 beats per minute, and respiratory rate 28 breaths per minute, with an oxygen saturation of 100% while breathing supplemental oxygen from a non-rebreather mask. There was pain on passive flexion and extension of the neck , and the neck was supple with rotation. The jugulovenous pressure was 6 cm. The lungs revealed mild crackles at the left base, and breath sounds were otherwise clear. A 2/6 systolic murmur was heard loudest at the base. The patient was able to follow simple commands, but speech was not fluent. Testing for cranial nerves III-XII, strength and sensation was normal, as was the remainder of the examination.
Studies
The level of D-dimer, drawn at the other hospital, was reportedly elevated. The white blood cells measured 17,700 cells per cubic millimeter (94% polymorphonuclear leukocytes, 4% lymphocytes, and 2% monocytes ) and levels of hemoglobin and platelets were normal. An electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus tachycardia, a prolonged QT interval for the rate, and diffuse ST segment and T wave abnormalities .
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Initial medications included atrovent and albuterol nebulizers, methylprednisolone (125 mg intravenously), crystalloid intravenous fluid 1.5 liters, and nitroglycerin by continuous intravenous drip. Computed tomography, performed according to a pulmonary embolism protocol, was negative.
A lumbar puncture was performed, and analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) revealed yellow, moderately turbid fluid with 1823 white cells (77% neutrophils, 21% lymphocytes, and 2% monocytes) and 433 red cells per cubic millimeter in the 1st tube, and 1188 white cells and 175 red cells per cubic millimeter in the 4th tube. The level of glucose was 124 mg/dl (reference range 50-75 mg/dl) and protein 505 mg/dl (ref. range 5-55 mg/dl). Stain of the cytospin of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is shown in Figures 1 and 2 .
Additional ceftriaxone (2 grams) was administered, and acyclovir (600 mg), vancomycin (1 g), and ampicillin (2 g) were added.

Treatment and Followup
Broad spectrum antibiotics were administered pending the results of the CSF and blood cultures, when gentamicin was added. The patient's temperature decreased to below 100°F (37.8°C); her mental status improved; and the acyclovir, ceftriaxone, and vancomycin were stopped. The patient declined to have a repeat lumbar puncture despite continued complaints of mild headache. The ampicillin and gentamicin were continued, with a plan to treat for 2.5-3 weeks intravenously. She developed increased cardiac enzymes and associated ST elevations, which were thought to be caused by increased myocardial demand in the setting of tachycardia and infection. A beta-blocker was added to her medications. She gradually improved, and was discharged on the 15th hospital day to a rehabilitation facility to complete her course of antibiotic therapy. At follow-up in Infectious Disease clinic one month after discharge, she had returned to her baseline health and felt well.
Discussion
Listeria monocytogenes , a gram-positive bacillus and facultative anaerobe, is one of the leading causes of community-acquired meningitis in adults older than 60 years of age, infants younger than 1 month, pregnant women and immunocompromised hosts, with the highest incidence of L. monocytogenes infection occurring in the first two groups. L. monocytogenes is ubiquitous in nature, and has been isolated from many foods including, but not limited to, soft cheeses, processed delicatessen meats, and raw vegetables. Consumption of contaminated food is thought to represent the mode of transmission in most patients; vertical transmission from mother to child during or after childbirth is also thought to be responsible for neonatal infections. Incubation periods are long and may range from 11 to 70 days [Lorber 2005, Tunkel and Scheld 2005]; therefore, in isolated cases it may be difficult to identify the exact source of the bacteria.
L. monocytogenes is the only species of Listeria that is pathogenic in humans. Presenting symptoms of L. monocytogenes meningitis may include fever, altered mental status and/or headache. In one review, all patients with documented L. monocytogenes meningitis had at least one of the symptoms described above, and 28% had all three [Mylonakis et al 1998]. Listerial infection of the central nervous system may also present as an abscess and be mistaken for primary or metatstatic malignancy. Rarely a rhombencephalitis of the brainstem occurs. In these cases, CSF may be very bland, and even culture-negative.
In this patient, gram-positive bacilli were seen on the stain of cerebrospinal fluid, and the diagnosis of L. monocytogenes meningitis confirmed by culture results. However, in other clinical specimens, the organism may have a coccoid, diplococcoid or diphtheroid appearance with variable colorization on gram staining [Lorber 2005] and, consequently, identifying the causative agent may be more difficult until specific culture results are known.
Although L. monocytogenes is only the 5th leading cause of community acquired meningitis, it has the highest mortality rate [Lorber 2005, Tunkel and Scheld 2005]. The organism’s pattern of resistance to cephalosporins, a common empiric therapy for meningitis, may contribute to its high mortality rate. Consequently, it is important to consider this entity and administer appropriate antibiotics, which may include penicillin or ampicillin as well as the addition of gentamicin, in relevant patients.
Final Diagnosis
Meningitis caused by Listeria monocytogenes , and acute myocardial infarction in an elderly patient.
