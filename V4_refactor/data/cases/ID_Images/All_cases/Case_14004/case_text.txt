History of Present Illness
2014 September-October Featured Case: A man in his forties with recent headache, cough, chills and diaphoresis, followed by a rash, was seen in a medical walk-in clinic.
The patient was well until 5 days before presentation when, in mid-spring , headache associated with chills, diaphoresis and sensitivity to sound occurred. He took ibuprofen for his symptoms, with some improvement. Two days later, a non-productive cough and a mildly itchy throat developed. The next day, his headache improved, and his eyes became watery and red . The following day, he flew to New England from his home in the Midwest. On the morning of the 5th day after the onset of symptoms , he awakened with a rash on his face and upper torso, without pruritus or pain ; his other symptoms had improved.
He came to a medical walk-in clinic for evaluation. He reported dark-colored urine despite drinking 'lots of water'. The remainder of a review of systems was negative.

A nasal specimen was positive for measles virus by direct immunofluorescent antibody testing ( Figures 3 and 4 ). On the second day, testing for Measles virus IgG antibodies was positive . The next day, testing for Measles virus IgM antibodies was positive, as reported by an outside laboratory.
Rapid test for influenza A and B and streptococcal pharyngitis were negative. Testing for hepatitis A was consistent with prior exposure or vaccination; testing for hepatitis B and C viruses was negative; testing for typhus IgG antibodies was 1:128 and was thought to be consistent with past disease. Treatment and Followup
Upon initial evaluation, the patient was immediately placed on airborne (respiratory) precautions and, pending the results of diagnostic tests, placed in a negative pressure room upon transfer to the emergency department and when admitted to the hospital. The hospital infection control epidemiologist and local and state public health authorities were contacted regarding the suspicion of a diagnosis of measles, and contact exposure investigation was performed. Epidemiologic investigation eventually linked this patient to a person traveling from another country within the previous weeks (and before his travel to New England), who was at the same airport gate, but not on the same plane, and was diagnosed with measles. The measles genotype of both patients was the same (genotype D8).
Ibuprofen was administered as needed for headache. The patient was discharged on the sixth hospital day.
Discussion
Measles is caused by a single-stranded enveloped RNA virus, a member of the Morbillivirus genus in the Paramyxoviridae family. The virus is highly contagious through respiratory secretions via coughing and sneezing. The period of contagiousness is up to five days before the appearance of rash and within four-to-five days afterwards. Classic measles has four clinical stages. First, the incubation period starts after measles enters the respiratory mucosa or conjunctivae, replicates, and spreads to reticuloendothelial sites. This period lasts 8-10 days. Secondly, the prodrome phase is characterized by fever and malaise, followed by cough, coryza, and conjunctivitis. This period usually lasts 2-3 days. Patients may manifest Koplik’s spots (white spots on the buccal mucosa), which are pathognomonic. The third stage is the exanthema, which is a blanching maculopapular rash starting on the face and spreading inferiorly to the trunk and extremities. Palms and soles are usually spared. Clinical improvement starts within 48 hours of the appearance of the rash. Complications include pneumonia, encephalitis, gingivostomatitis, gastroenteritis, hepatitis, and myocarditis. The fourth phase, recovery, is marked usually by lifelong immunity.
Measles remains a global public health problem with 120,000 deaths caused by the disease in 2012 [WHO, 2014]. Endemic measles in the United States was eliminated in 2000 [Katz et al, 2004]. However, during 2001-2008, there were 557 confirmed cases of measles and 38 outbreaks [Fiebelkorn et al, 2010]. Of these cases, 42% were directly imported from 44 countries, and the rest were US-acquired cases with either a link to an imported case or with unclear source (i.e. no established link to imported case or endemic transmission). Sixty-five percent of cases during this period were in unvaccinated patients, and vaccination status was unknown in 18% of cases. Two deaths were reported. The acute clinical syndrome may be very severe in adults without prior immunity.
Importation of measles cases from endemic areas of the world continues to occur, with a record number of measles cases reported in the US this year from January 1 to May 23, 2014. The Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (MMWR) noted 228 confirmed cases during that period with fifteen outbreaks accounting for 79% of the cases [Gastañaduy et al, 2014]. Unvaccinated (69%) and those with unknown vaccination status (20%) again accounted for the majority of cases. Forty-five direct importations from abroad have been reported, and 49% of these importations were travelers returning from the Philippines, where a large outbreak has been occurring since October 2013.
Because of the limited experience that newer generations of US health care providers have had with measles, cases have been initially misdiagnosed as Kawasaki disease, dengue, and scarlet fever. There has also been a report of measles outbreak among persons with prior evidence of immunity [Rosen et al, 2014]. In cases of previously vaccinated patients who develop incomplete immunity, a “vaccine-modified measles” can occur, which is a milder form with a shorter prodromal period. The exanthem often does not progress to confluence, and Koplik’s spots may not appear [Cherry 1993; Choe et al, 2012]. These trends underscore the importance of maintaining high population immunity and vigilance for measles. Two doses of MMR vaccine is 95% effective in preventing measles [Marin et al, 2006].
Suspicion for measles requires immediate respiratory isolation of the patient, including potential hospitalization, as well as notification of relevant public health authorities. While these precautions are necessary for minimizing the risk of an outbreak, they create a considerable amount of work for clinical staff and inconvenience for the patient. Nevertheless, given the highly contagious nature of measles and the potential for significant morbidity and mortality in individuals without adequate immunization, clinicians should be vigilant in considering the diagnosis of measles in at-risk patients who present with suggestive signs and symptoms and should isolate such patients while diagnostic testing is undertaken.
Of the other pathogens on the differential, the rash of secondary syphilis is highly varied, but classically is a macular lesion on the palms and/or soles [Cohen et al, 2013]. Scarlet fever produces a diffuse erythematous rash with “sandpaper” quality to the skin due to small 1-2mm papular elevations. Classically, there is circumoral pallor [Cherry 1993]. Rocky Mountain spotted fever (RMSF), caused by Rickettsia rickettsii , leads to a macular blanching erythematous rash that becomes petechial over time, beginning on the ankles and wrists, spreading to the trunk [Woods 2013]. The rash of rubella is similar in distribution to measles, but the spread is more rapid, and the color is less intense. Suboccipital and postauricular lymphadenopathy are particularly common in adults with rubella, and can help with the diagnosis [Cherry 1993]. Other diagnoses to consider include acute HIV (which can present with a maculopapular rash, often involving the upper thorax, collar, and face, and sometimes the scalp and extremities) and parvovirus infection, which is usually seen in school-aged children and usually causes an erythematous malar rash over the cheeks (“slapped cheek”), followed by lacelike rash on trunk and extremities.
Final Diagnosis
Acute measles infection.
